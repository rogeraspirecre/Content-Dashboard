## The verdicts
- **Brand new business, experienced owner, lots of cash: GREEN FLAG.** But make sure the experienced person with the money is on the lease, not just the new entity. If it's only the entity and things go badly, that person walks and you're left with nothing.
- **Successful in another state, opening their first location here: RED FLAG.** Doesn't mean don't do the deal, but what works in one state often doesn't work in another. First location in your state with few other locations is a higher risk deal.
- **Wants to fund an expensive build out themselves: GREEN FLAG.** "There's few things a landlord loves more than a tenant putting their own money into your building."
- **Strong financials but asks for a short lease: GREEN FLAG.** Charge a premium for the short term. Since they won't commit long, you won't put much or any capital into tenant improvements and concessions. They may end up staying long term anyway.
- **Wants protection from a competitor moving into the space: DEPENDS.** Reasonable relative to their size is a green flag. Asking for exclusivity on 100 square feet in a million square foot project is a red flag, and they're probably going to be a huge pain as a tenant.

## YouTube description
Green flag or red flag, tenant edition.

A brand new business with an experienced owner and a lot of cash: green flag. Just make sure the person with the experience and the money is responsible for the lease, not only the new entity. If it's just the entity and things don't work out, that person can walk away and you're left with nothing. A business that was successful in another state and is opening its first location here: red flag. That doesn't mean you shouldn't do the deal, but what works in one state often doesn't work in another, and a first location with few others behind it is a higher risk deal.

A tenant who wants to fund an expensive build out themselves: green flag. There are few things a landlord loves more than a tenant putting their own money into your building. Strong financials but they want a short lease: green flag. Charge a premium for the short term, and since they aren't committing long term, you won't have to put nearly as much capital into tenant improvements and concessions. They might end up staying long term anyway.

And a tenant who wants protection from a competitor moving into the space: it depends on how large they are relative to the property. If it's reasonable given their size, green flag. If they're leasing 100 square feet in a million square foot project, that's a red flag, and they're probably going to be a huge pain to deal with.

## EDIT NOTES
- **Lead with the short lease verdict, close on the 100 square feet line.** Contrarian first, funniest last. The current order buries both.
- **The guarantee point is the most valuable thing here and it's easy to miss.** Add on-screen text: "Get the person on the lease, not just the entity." That's the line a landlord will act on tomorrow.
- **Number five is really two verdicts.** Reasonable exclusivity is green, disproportionate exclusivity is red. Show both cards on screen or it reads as a dodge.
- **Title carefully.** Tenant quality and green/red flag are both well covered on the channel already. What's new here is the landlord's seat during lease negotiation, so lean on that framing.
- **Reach expectation:** landlord-facing, so the green/red flag range of 11K to 17K rather than the broad rankings. The format does the work; don't expect ranking numbers.
- **Natural Pt. 2, all real requests landlords get:** wants a free rent period, wants an early termination clause, asks to sublease, wants signage rights, asks for a right of first refusal on the space next door, wants you to buy out their old lease, franchisee with one location, tenant paying a year up front.
