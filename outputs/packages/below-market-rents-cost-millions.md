## The argument
- The number one way owners leave money on the table is being afraid tenants will leave.
- Happens most with owners who've held a property a long time. They bought cheap, they're used to the cash flow.
- At renewal the tenant says: raise my rent and I'm gone. The owner is doing well, so they renew below market.
- The truth: if the owner understood the market, they'd know rents are 20, 30, 40% higher than what that tenant pays, leasing demand is strong, and vacancy is low.
- If that tenant went looking for comparable space at what they're paying, they wouldn't find anything.
- Raising the rent isn't taking advantage of them. It's charging a fair price for the product you provide, which is space for their business to operate in.
- Commercial property sells on a multiple of net operating income. Below-market rents mean selling for sometimes millions less than you could have.

## YouTube description
The number one way commercial real estate owners leave money on the table is being afraid their tenants are going to leave. This especially happens with owners who have held a property for a very long time. They bought it for a low amount compared to today's pricing, and they're used to all the cash flow from tenants paying rent all these years.

Every time those tenants come up for renewal, they say: if you raise my rent, I'm going to leave. And since the owner is doing well and is happy with the cash flow, they listen, and they renew the lease for less than market rate. But if that owner understood the market, they'd realize rents are 20, 30, even 40% higher than what that tenant is paying, that leasing demand is strong, and that vacancy is low. If that tenant went out and looked for comparable space at the rate they're paying, they wouldn't find anything.

You could have increased that tenant's rent, and you wouldn't have been taking advantage of them. You'd have been charging a fair price for the product you provide, which is space for their business to operate in. Commercial properties sell based on a multiple of the net operating income. So when your rents aren't near where they could be, you're going to sell that property for sometimes millions of dollars less than you could have.

## EDIT NOTES
- **Open on the threat.** "If you raise my rent, I'm going to leave" is dialogue, and dialogue beats explanation as a hook. Then take it apart.
- **This needs the same graphic treatment as the free rent clip.** Show rent under market, then the NOI, then the sale price at a cap rate. The claim is "millions," so give the viewer the arithmetic.
- **Fair-price framing is the most defensible part.** "You wouldn't have been taking advantage of that tenant" is what keeps this from sounding like landlord greed. Keep it, and consider putting it on screen.
- **Watch the tension with the retention clips.** You have seven or more videos on keeping tenants happy, and this one says don't fear losing them. They aren't in conflict — happy doesn't mean underpriced — but someone will say so in comments. The reply: a good tenant at market rent is the goal, and a tenant who only stays because they're 30% under market was never loyal, just well priced.
- **Pairs naturally with the free rent clip.** Both are about how rent drives value through NOI. Together they make a strong two-part series on why what you charge matters more than what you collect this month.
- **Best lead-gen clip in the owner lane.** The viewer is a long-time owner who may be under-rented, which is exactly who hires a broker to run a renewal or a sale. Worth an ad test.
