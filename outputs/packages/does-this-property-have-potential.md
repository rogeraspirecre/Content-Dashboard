## The framework
1. **What is the property used for?** Start with product type.
2. **Office:** what are office rents, and what would you have to put into the building to attract a tenant? If you spend that money, would a tenant really come, or are you hoping for a miracle?
3. **Industrial:** what's the access, what's the demand, what are the rates?
4. **Retail:** same drill. Understand the product type, the economics, and the demand for it.
5. **What does it cost to buy, and what would you have to put into it** to get it to where you can lease it or sell it?
6. **Is there a spread there?**
7. **Is the area going in a positive or negative direction?**
- It's not a simple question. It takes a lot of data points and context, and that's why good real estate investors win: they know which data points matter and how to judge whether a property has potential.

## YouTube description
Evaluating a property requires understanding what's going on in the area and understanding the market.

First things first: what is the property used for? If it's an office building, what are office rents, and what would you have to put into the building to attract a tenant? And if you put that money in, would a tenant really come, or are you just hoping for a miracle? If it's industrial, what's the access, what's the demand, and what are the rates? If it's retail, same thing. You have to understand the product type, the economics, and the demand for that product type.

Then: what does the property cost to buy, and what would you have to put into it to get it to a place where you can rent it out or sell it to someone else? Is there a spread there? And is the area going in a positive direction or a negative one?

It's not a simple question. Evaluating a property requires a lot of different data points and context, and that's why good real estate investors win. They know which data points matter and how to determine whether a property has potential.
